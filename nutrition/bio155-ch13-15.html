Chapter 13 · BIO-155 Nutrition

Weight Management

What body weight is made of, how we measure fat, where energy goes, and what drives us to eat.

MapHow this chapter fits together

The chapter tells one story in four steps. First, what is the problem? Excess body fat, especially fat around the organs, raises disease risk. Second, how do we measure it? BMI is quick but crude, so we also measure body composition directly. Third, why does fat build up? Energy in versus energy out. Fourth, why is that balance so hard to control? Genes, hormones, and environment push on appetite. Treatment comes last because it has to answer all of the above.

13.1Overweight vs obesity

These two words are not interchangeable. Overweight means extra weight from anything: bone, muscle, body fat, or body water. Obesity means excessive body fat specifically. A bodybuilder can be overweight without being obese.

Water is about 60% of an adult's body weight. That is why the scale can jump two or three pounds overnight after a salty meal: that is fluid, not fat.

Memory HookOverweight weighs everything on the scale. Obesity only counts the fat.

Why excess fat is dangerous

Fat first fills adipose tissue. When that storage is full, fat migrates into the abdomen, heart, and liver. Fat in those places disrupts metabolism and produces a cluster of problems: insulin resistance + abnormal blood lipids + high blood pressure = metabolic syndrome.

Central obesity

May raise heart attack and stroke risk as much as the three leading risk factors: high LDL cholesterol, hypertension, and smoking.

PCOS (in females with obesity)

Irregular or absent periods, low estrogen, high testosterone, ovarian cysts, and insulin resistance. PCOS can lead to infertility.

Memory HookThe three leading risk factors spell LHS: LDL, Hypertension, Smoking. Central fat is as bad as all of them.

Weight stigma

Stigma in health care causes psychological distress and makes people avoid health-care services. It also feeds binge eating, less activity, more weight gain, isolation, depression, and low self-esteem. Notice the loop: stigma is meant to "motivate" but actually makes the weight problem worse.

Quick check: Insulin resistance shows up in two conditions in this section. Which two?
Metabolic syndrome and PCOS. Both tie excess fat to hormone problems.

13.2BMI: the quick screen

Body mass index compares weight to height:

$$\text{BMI}=\frac{\text{weight in kg}}{(\text{height in m})^2}$$

Converting units first

The formula only works in kilograms and meters. If you are given pounds, feet, inches, or centimeters, convert before you plug in.

What BMI cannot tell you

BMI does not describe body composition. It cannot tell muscle from fat, so a very muscular person can be classified "overweight." It is less useful for highly muscular people, older adults, and people with chronic illnesses, and it ignores sex, race, and ethnicity.

The flip sideNormal weight obesity is a normal BMI with an elevated percent body fat. These people look "fine" on BMI but carry higher cardiovascular disease and mortality risk. This is exactly why the next section measures fat directly.

13.3Body composition and measuring body fat

Body composition is the measurement of body tissues, usually expressed as percent body fat. The body splits into two compartments:

Fat mass
All adipose tissue. Includes essential fat, the fat needed for body processes to occur normally. Females carry more essential fat for reproductive functions.
Lean (fat-free) mass
Total body weight minus adipose tissue weight: muscle, bone, organs, water.
Subcutaneous fat
Fat under the skin. Insulates and protects. (sub = under, cutaneous = skin)
Visceral fat
Fat between the internal organs. (viscera = organs) The dangerous kind.
Body Fat Classification
Memory HookLearn the male ladder (13-21, 22-25, 26-31, 32+), then add about 10 for females (23-31, 32-37, 38-42, 43+). The gap is essential fat.

13.4Where the fat sits

How much fat matters, but where it sits matters just as much. Excess visceral fat around the abdominal organs is central obesity (upper-body fat), linked to heart disease, cancers, and diabetes. This connects back to 13.1: central fat is the fat that migrates into organs and causes metabolic syndrome.

Click a Shape
Click the apple or the pear.
Memory HookAn apple's core is in the middle, like fat packed around the organs. A pear is heavy at the bottom, like fat on hips and thighs. Males = apple, females = pear.

Waist circumference is the quick way to check central fat: the tape crosses just above the upper hip bones, snug but not compressing the skin.

13.5Energy balance

Energy balance compares energy in (food, beverages, alcohol) with energy out (the calories your cells use). Energy out has three parts:

Basal metabolism
Up to 2/3 of the day. Breathing, heartbeat, marrow making red blood cells, kidneys filtering. BMR is the rate of energy use for life-sustaining activities and does not include skeletal muscle movement or digestion.
Physical activity
The most variable and most changeable part. Longer, more frequent, more intense movement burns more. Includes NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis): fidgeting, shivering, cooking, cleaning, holding upright posture.
Thermic effect of food
About 5 to 10% of intake: energy to digest food and beverages and to absorb and further process the nutrients (GI contractions, digestive juices).
Memory HookEnergy out is a BAT: Basal (biggest), Activity (most variable), Thermic (tiny, 5 to 10%).
Build a Day of Energy Out

BMR vs RMR, and what moves BMR

RMR (resting metabolic rate) is measured after resting and eating, so it runs about 10% higher than BMR.

Raises BMR

Fever, stress, growth, caffeine, smoking, hormones (such as thyroid hormone)

Lowers BMR

Aging, fasting or starvation, malnutrition

Connect forward: "Starvation lowers BMR" is the same fact you will see in Chapter 14, where anorexia slows BMR as the body tries to conserve energy. A slower BMR is also why very low calorie diets stall.

Positive vs negative balance and the 3,500 rule

Positive balance

Intake greater than output, so weight increases. Examples: pregnancy, body building.

Negative balance

Intake less than output, so weight is lost. Example: chronic disease.

Rule of thumb: about 3,500 kcal eaten in excess stores about 1 pound of body fat, and the reverse for losing it.

$$500\ \tfrac{\text{kcal}}{\text{day}}\times 7\ \text{days}=3{,}500\ \text{kcal}\approx 1\ \text{lb per week}$$

Quick check: Someone cuts 250 kcal per day. About how long until they lose 1 pound?
$3{,}500 \div 250 = 14$ days, so about 2 weeks.

13.6Genes, hormones, and behavior

If weight were only math, everyone would balance the equation. It is hard because biology and surroundings push on the "energy in" side. More than 300 sites in the human genome are linked to obesity and fat distribution, and an obesogenic environment (all the daily circumstances that push us toward fatness) works on those genes.

Hunger
The physiological sensation that drives eating.
Appetite
The desire for appealing food. You can have appetite without hunger (dessert after a big meal).
Satiety
The sense that enough food or beverage has been consumed.
Memory HookHunger is the body, appetite is the mind, satiety sounds like "satisfied."

The hypothalamus is the control center. It collects nervous, endocrine, and digestive signals. Click each organ to follow its hormone.

Hunger and Satiety Signals
Click the stomach, the gut, or the fat tissue.
Memory HookGhrelin is the stomach growling. Leptin comes from the lipids (fat tissue). PYY tells ghrelin to "pipe down."
Common trapPeople with obesity usually have plenty of leptin. The problem is leptin resistance: the brain stops listening to the signal. Do not answer "leptin deficiency."

Connect forward: Leptin is made by fat tissue, so very low body fat means very low leptin. That is part of why the low energy availability in Chapter 14 (female athlete triad) shuts down reproductive hormones.

13.7Treatment

Successful weight management combines three things, one for each piece of the story above: dietary changes (energy in), physical activity (energy out), and psychological and behavior changes such as better sleep patterns (the hormones and environment).

The activity target paired with diet: at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity per week, plus fewer sedentary behaviors.

Memory Hook150 or 75: vigorous minutes count double. The same numbers return in Chapter 15 as the adult activity guideline.
Fad dietsThey tend to be unsustainable and nutritionally inadequate, ignore dietary recommendations, and lead to weight regain.

Disordered eating

Behaviors that are neither normal nor healthy: restrained eating, fasting, repeating the same meal, an occasional binge or purge. Does not meet DSM-5 criteria.

Eating disorder

A disturbance in eating behavior that jeopardizes physical or psychological health and meets DSM-5 criteria.

DSM-5 = Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Its three main eating disorders: anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder.

Memory HookDisordered eating is the hallway. An eating disorder is the room with a diagnosis on the door. The big three go A, B, B: Anorexia, Bulimia, Binge-eating.

14.3Anorexia nervosa

an = without, orex = mouth or appetite, nervosa = of nervous origin. The person is starving themselves.

Core features: refusal to maintain a minimally normal body weight, plus a distorted perception of body shape and weight. They see themselves as fat while dangerously underweight.

The body is malnourished: deprived of lean protein and body fat. Almost every effect makes sense as the body conserving energy or running out of materials. Click a body system.

Do not mix these upMenarche is the first period, which low estrogen delays. Amenorrhea is no menstruation for 3+ months (a = without, men = monthly, rrhea = flow).

Anorexia often occurs with depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Young adults with anorexia have a mortality rate about 12 times their peers, usually from cardiac complications or suicide.

Connect back: "BMR slows" is the Chapter 13 fact that fasting and starvation lower BMR. Less fat also means less leptin, and less insulation.

14.4Bulimia nervosa

Pattern: binge eating followed by purging (self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, or excessive exercise) to rid the body of the kilocalories consumed. People with bulimia are usually normal weight or overweight, think constantly about food, and keep the behavior secret. That is why it is easy to miss.

14.5Binge-eating disorder

Recurring binges with a marked feeling of lack of control, but the person rarely or never purges, so obesity risk rises. It is the most common eating disorder in the U.S., twice as common in females. It raises risk for obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension, plus self-loathing, depression, and anxiety. Cognitive behavioral therapy combined with lifestyle recommendations appears beneficial.

14.7Female athlete triad and RED-S

The triad is the interplay of three things: energy availability (low energy, negative energy balance), menstrual function, and bone mineral density. At-risk sports reward low body mass: gymnastics, dance, cheerleading, distance running.

Step Through the Chain
It starts with energy.
Memory HookTriad = EMB: Energy, Menstruation, Bones. Osteo-penia = bone poverty.
Triad vs RED-SRED-S = Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport. It is the broader syndrome: it affects both males and females and harms health and performance. The triad is the female-only version focused on three outcomes.

Connect: "Low energy availability" is Chapter 13's negative energy balance in an athlete. Chapter 15 adds the other side: athletes need enough total kcal and carbohydrate, and female athletes are also at higher risk of iron deficiency.

14.8Treatment and prevention

Treatment takes a multidisciplinary team: nutrition, mental health, medicine, sometimes pharmacy.

Cognitive behavioral therapy

Challenges current beliefs and replaces them with more realistic thoughts.

Dialectical behavioral therapy

Builds skills for handling stress and feelings of inadequacy.

Memory HookCBT changes the Cognitions (thoughts). DBT builds the Deal-with-it skills.

Other therapies: phototherapy (light), biofeedback (recognizing warning signs, heart rate monitoring), equine therapy (self, relationships, fun), meditation and relaxation therapy, and acupuncture (for depression and anxiety).

Prevention: early identification, education to improve body image, and teaching athletes, parents, and coaches about the triad.

15.1Basics and guidelines

Physical activity
Bodily movement from muscle contractions that significantly raises energy expenditure.
Exercise
Planned, structured, repetitive movement done to promote or maintain fitness.
Physical fitness
The result. Physical activity is the broadest, exercise is the planned subset, fitness is the outcome.
Aerobic
Sustained, rhythmic contractions of large muscle groups.
Resistance
Builds muscle strength and endurance (and mass).

Adult guideline: at least 150 min/week moderate OR 75 min/week vigorous aerobic activity, plus muscle strengthening on at least 2 days/week. These are the same 150/75 numbers from Chapter 13's weight-loss target.

Session order: warm-up, peak activity, cool-down. The cool-down lets heart rate slow gradually and reduces cramping.

15.2Fuels and glycogen

Glucose is stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles. During exercise the liver breaks its glycogen down and releases glucose to the blood. Total glycogen holds less than 2,000 kcal, roughly 20 miles of running. When it runs out, muscles fatigue. This is "hitting the wall."

Fuel Mix by Intensity
Memory HookSprint on sugar, stroll on fat. High intensity: ATP mainly from carbohydrate. Moderate intensity: ATP from fatty acid oxidation.

Trained athletes oxidize more fat at the same intensity, sparing glycogen so they can go longer.

Energy needs: male endurance athletes training more than 90 min/day need at least 50 kcal/kg/day; females need 45 to 50 kcal/kg/day.

15.3Carbohydrate

TrainingCarbohydrate
1 to 3 h/day, moderate to high intensity6 to 10 g/kg/day
More than 4 to 5 h/day8 to 12 g/kg/day
Carbohydrate Calculator

Carbohydrate loading manipulates training and carb intake to maximize muscle glycogen stores. It delays fatigue in events lasting longer than 90 minutes. A healthy person stores about 6 g of glycogen per kg, and every gram of glycogen pulls about 3 g of water into the muscle with it, which is why loaded athletes feel heavy.

15.4Fat and protein

Athletes should get 20 to 35% of energy from fat, saturated fat under 10%. Dropping fat below 20% risks inadequate energy and nutrients.

Protein is not a major fuel. Carbohydrate spares protein: with too little carb (or too much fat), amino acids get converted to glucose, because fatty acids can never provide glucose.

Memory HookFat cannot be turned into glucose, so when carbs run short the body raids muscle protein. Carbs are the bodyguard for protein.
Do not mix these upDOMS (delayed-onset muscle soreness) comes from microtears in muscle fibers and hits 24 to 48 hours after activity. The burning during activity is lactic acid, a different thing.

Protein pattern: 20 to 30 g of high-quality protein at each of 4 to 5 small meals. Even the highest recommendations can be met without protein supplements.

15.5Micronutrients and iron

Routine antioxidant vitamin supplements are NOT recommended for athletes. Eat antioxidant-rich foods instead.

Iron is part of hemoglobin, which delivers oxygen for energy metabolism. Anemia means tiring easily and poor aerobic performance. Risk runs high in females, distance runners, and athletes on low-calorie or plant-based diets.

Iron-deficiency anemia

True shortage of iron. Real loss of oxygen delivery and performance.

Sports anemia

NOT true iron deficiency. A temporary, adaptive response to endurance training: older, fragile red cells get destroyed.

15.6Fluids, heat illness, sports drinks

Three heat illnesses, mildest to worst: exercise-associated muscle cramps, heat exhaustion, heatstroke. Heatstroke means a body temperature above 104°F and is potentially fatal.

Memory HookC, E, S climbing a ladder: Cramps, Exhaustion, Stroke. The top rung is above 104.
Sweat Loss Check (2% Rule)

Electrolytes lost in sweat: sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium. A normal diet replaces them for events under an hour.

15.7Ergogenic aids

An ergogenic aid is a substance or technique used to try to enhance physical performance ("ergo" = work, "genic" = gives rise to).

Anabolic steroids
The most dangerous and illegal class, derived from testosterone.
Creatine
Combines with phosphate to make creatine phosphate in muscle. Supported for short-term, repetitive, high-intensity work like lifting and sprinting.
Caffeine
Enhances alertness and endurance but causes GI upset, nervousness, headaches. NCAA restricts it, capping urine concentration at 15 mg/L.
