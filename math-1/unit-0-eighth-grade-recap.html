AFunctions

This is the single most important strand for Math 1. Almost the entire course is the word "function" getting progressively more specific.

What a function is

A function is a rule that assigns to each input exactly one output. That is the whole definition. An input is never allowed two different outputs.

The test on a graph is the vertical line test: if any vertical line crosses the graph twice, some input has two outputs, so it is not a function. The test on a table is simpler — look for a repeated input with different outputs.

Function notation

$f(3) = 7$ means "the input 3 produces the output 7". It is not $f$ times 3. Getting comfortable reading this now saves a lot of confusion later, because Math 1 writes almost everything this way.

Rate of change and initial value

In $y = mx + b$:  $m$ is the rate of change (how much $y$ moves when $x$ goes up by 1) and $b$ is the initial value (the output when $x = 0$).

Linear or not

A relationship is linear when the rate of change is constant — equal steps in the input always produce equal steps in the output. In a table, subtract neighbouring outputs and see whether you always get the same number.

Which of these tables is linear?
Table P: inputs 1, 2, 3, 4 → outputs 6, 10, 14, 18
Table Q: inputs 1, 2, 3, 4 → outputs 2, 4, 8, 16
P is linear. Differences are 4, 4, 4 — constant. Its rule is $y = 4x + 2$.
Q is not linear. Differences are 2, 4, 8 — growing. But its ratios are 2, 2, 2, which makes it exponential.
Holding on to both tests, differences and ratios, is exactly what Unit 1 asks you to do.
Where this goes Sequences in Unit 1 are functions whose inputs are position numbers. The "constant difference" test you just used is the test for an arithmetic sequence, and the "constant ratio" test is the test for a geometric one.

BLinear equations, slope, and solving

Slope from two points

$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

Change in output over change in input. Keep the order consistent — whichever point you call "2" in the top, use in the bottom too.

Through $(2, 3)$ and $(6, 11)$:
$m = \dfrac{11 - 3}{6 - 2} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2$
Then find $b$: substitute a point into $y = 2x + b$. Using $(2,3)$: $3 = 4 + b$, so $b = -1$.
Equation: $y = 2x - 1$

Solving equations in one variable

The rule that never changes: whatever you do to one side, do to the other. Work toward getting the variable alone.

Solve $3(x + 2) = 5x - 4$
$3x + 6 = 5x - 4$   distribute
$6 = 2x - 4$   subtract $3x$
$10 = 2x$   add 4
$x = 5$   divide by 2
Check: $3(7) = 21$ and $5(5) - 4 = 21$. Equal, so it is right.
Two answers that are not numbers If everything cancels and you are left with something true like $5 = 5$, every number works — infinitely many solutions. If you are left with something false like $5 = 7$, nothing works — no solution. Both are legitimate answers, not signs you made a mistake.
Solve $4x - 7 = 21$, then solve $\frac{2}{3}x + 5 = 11$.
$4x - 7 = 21 \Rightarrow 4x = 28 \Rightarrow x = 7$
$\frac{2}{3}x + 5 = 11 \Rightarrow \frac{2}{3}x = 6 \Rightarrow x = 6 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 9$
Where this goes Solving for $n$ in Unit 1 ("which row has 94 seats?") is exactly this skill. Unit 4 formalises it and adds inequalities.

CExponents and roots

Small topic, oversized consequences. Every exponential function in Math 1 is unreadable without these.

$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$
same base, multiply → add the exponents
$\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$
same base, divide → subtract the exponents
$(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$
power of a power → multiply the exponents
$a^0 = 1$
any nonzero base to the zero power is 1
$a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$
a negative exponent means reciprocal, not a negative answer
The two everyone gets wrong $a^0 = 1$, not 0. And $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8}$, not $-8$ — a negative exponent flips the number over, it never makes it negative.
Exponent rules drill

Square and cube roots

$\sqrt{p}$ undoes squaring and $\sqrt[3]{p}$ undoes cubing. Solving $x^2 = 49$ gives $x = 7$ or $x = -7$, because both square to 49. Solving $x^3 = 27$ gives only $x = 3$.

Even roots give two answers, odd roots give one. That asymmetry comes back in Unit 1 when you fill gaps in a geometric sequence, and again in Unit 6 when you solve quadratics.

Where this goes $r^0 = 1$ is what makes $a_1 \cdot r^{\,n-1}$ produce the right first term in Unit 1. All the rules reappear in Unit 2 for exponential functions and in Unit 7 for expression structure.

DSystems of two linear equations

Two equations, two unknowns. The solution is the point that satisfies both at once — which on a graph is exactly where the two lines cross.

Two lines and their intersection
Solve algebraically: $y = 2x + 1$ and $y = -x + 7$
Both equal $y$, so set them equal: $2x + 1 = -x + 7$
$3x = 6$, so $x = 2$
Substitute back: $y = 2(2) + 1 = 5$
Solution: $(2, 5)$ — and that is where the lines cross on the graph.
When there is no crossing point Parallel lines (same slope, different intercept) never meet — no solution. Identical lines overlap everywhere — infinitely many solutions. Try it above by setting both slopes to 2.
Where this goes Unit 5 is this topic taken further — substitution and elimination instead of graphing, and equations not already in slope-intercept form.

EThe real number system

Every real number has a decimal expansion, and the kind of expansion tells you what kind of number it is.

Rational
a decimal that either terminates (0.75) or repeats ($0.\overline{3}$). Equivalently, writable as a fraction of integers.
Irrational
a decimal that never terminates and never repeats. Examples: $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$.

Nesting, from smallest out: natural numbers sit inside whole numbers, inside integers, inside rationals. The irrationals sit alongside the rationals, sharing no members, and together the two make up the reals.

Roots are not automatically irrational $\sqrt{16} = 4$, which is perfectly rational. Only roots of non-perfect squares like $\sqrt{2}$ or $\sqrt{20}$ are irrational. Check whether the number under the root is a perfect square before deciding.

To place $\sqrt{20}$ on a number line, find the perfect squares either side: $16 < 20 < 25$, so $4 < \sqrt{20} < 5$. Since 20 sits closer to 16 than to 25, the value lands just below the midpoint 4.5 — it is about 4.47.

FThe Pythagorean theorem and distance

For a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:  $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. The hypotenuse is always the side opposite the right angle, and always the longest.

The converse is just as useful: if $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ holds for three side lengths, the triangle must be right-angled.

Distance between two points is the Pythagorean theorem in disguise

Drawing the horizontal and vertical legs turns any two points into a right triangle, so the distance formula is not a new fact to memorise — it is the Pythagorean theorem with the legs written as coordinate differences.

Where this goes Math 1 uses this for coordinate geometry proofs — showing a quadrilateral is a rhombus by checking side lengths, or finding a point that partitions a segment.

GScatter plots and two-way tables

A scatter plot puts one dot per individual, using two measurements as coordinates. What you look for:

Direction
positive (both rise together), negative (one rises as the other falls), or none
Form
roughly linear, curved, or no pattern
Strength
how tightly the dots hug the pattern
Outliers
points sitting far away from everything else

When the pattern looks linear you can fit a line through it and use that line's equation to make predictions. The interpretation matters more than the arithmetic:

Slope in context: "for each additional unit of $x$, $y$ changes by about $m$."

Intercept in context: "when $x$ is zero, $y$ is about $b$" — which is sometimes meaningless in the real situation, and saying so is a correct answer.

Association is not cause Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer; neither causes the other. Math 1 asks about this explicitly.

A two-way table does the same job for categorical data, counting how many fall into each combination of two categories. Relative frequencies (converting counts to percentages of a row or column) reveal association that raw counts can hide.

Where this goes Unit 8 is this strand extended — correlation coefficients, residuals, and being more careful about what a linear model does and does not license you to claim.

HGeometry you can park for now

Three 8th grade geometry topics barely appear in Math 1 but return in force in Math 2. Worth knowing they exist, not worth drilling before Unit 1.

Transformations
translations, reflections, rotations, and dilations. Two figures are congruent if one maps onto the other using only the first three; similar if a dilation is allowed too.
Angle relationships
angles formed by parallel lines and a transversal; the angles of a triangle summing to 180 degrees; the exterior angle rule.
Volume
cylinders, cones, and spheres. The relationship worth remembering: a cone is exactly one third of the cylinder with the same base and height.

The one piece of this that does matter soon is dilations. Stretching a graph vertically is the same idea applied to functions, which is how Math 1 and Math 2 talk about transforming parent functions.

→The connection worth seeing before you start

Math 1 can feel like a pile of separate topics. It is not. Almost the whole first half is one idea seen from four angles, and knowing that in advance makes the sequencing feel deliberate instead of random.

8th grade gave you $y = mx + b$ — a constant rate of change, defined for every input.

Unit 1 takes the same rule and only lets you ask for whole-number positions. That is an arithmetic sequence: the common difference $d$ is the old rate of change $m$, and the terms are a handful of dots plucked off the line.

Unit 1 also introduces the multiplying version — geometric sequences — which are dots plucked off an exponential curve, even though you have not formally met one yet.

Unit 2 fills the gaps back in. It takes those two dotted patterns and lets the input be any number again, producing linear and exponential functions. Nothing new is invented; the domain is just reconnected.

The two columns of that table are the same objects with the holes filled in. When Unit 2 opens by asking you to compare a savings account that adds \$50 a month with one that grows 3% a month, you are looking at an arithmetic sequence and a geometric sequence that have been allowed to breathe.
