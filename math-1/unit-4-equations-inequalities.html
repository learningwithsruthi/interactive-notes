Equations & Inequalities

You already know how to solve. This unit is about being able to say why every step was allowed.

MapWhat this unit covers

Most of the mechanics here you met in 8th grade. What is new is the demand for justification — NC's own note on this standard says it is the first time a content standard requires you to explain your reasoning, not just get the answer.

The thread through the unit: a solution is a value that makes a statement true. Everything else — the algebra, the graphs, the shaded regions — is a different way of finding or displaying that same set of values.

L1Solving is reasoning, and reasoning can be written down

Every legal move in solving comes from one of a small set of properties. You are not expected to memorise their names, but you are expected to describe what you did and why it preserved the solution.

Addition property
adding the same quantity to both sides keeps the equation true
Multiplication property
multiplying both sides by the same nonzero number keeps it true
Distributive property
$a(b + c) = ab + ac$ — rewrites one side without changing its value
Combining like terms
also a rewrite of one side; the two sides stay equal because nothing was added or removed
Two different kinds of move Some steps change both sides (adding, multiplying). Others rewrite one side into an equivalent expression (distributing, combining like terms). Both are legal, for different reasons, and a good justification says which kind you just used.

The same equation, two valid routes

Solve $5(x + 3) - 3x = 55$. Both methods below are correct. Step through each and compare.

Step through a solution and see the justification for each line

Both routes reach $x = 20$, and both are fully justified. Method 1 is usually less error-prone because it avoids fractions; Method 2 is shorter when every term shares a factor. Being able to defend your choice is part of the standard — there is no single required method.

L2Solving linear equations

The goal never changes: isolate the variable using moves that keep both sides equal. A reliable order when you are unsure:

1. Clear fractions by multiplying both sides by a common denominator, if that helps.
2. Distribute to remove brackets.
3. Combine like terms on each side separately.
4. Collect variable terms on one side, constants on the other.
5. Divide by the coefficient.
6. Check by substituting back. This is not optional — it catches almost every arithmetic slip.
1. Solve $\dfrac{7}{3}y - 8 = 111$.
Add 8: $\dfrac{7}{3}y = 119$.
Multiply by $\dfrac{3}{7}$: $y = 119 \cdot \dfrac{3}{7} = 51$.
Check: $\dfrac{7}{3}(51) = 7 \cdot 17 = 119$, and $119 - 8 = 111$. Correct.
2. Solve $\dfrac{3 + x}{7} = \dfrac{x - 9}{4}$.
Cross-multiply (really: multiply both sides by 28): $4(3 + x) = 7(x - 9)$.
$12 + 4x = 7x - 63$
$75 = 3x$, so $x = 25$.
Check: $\dfrac{28}{7} = 4$ and $\dfrac{16}{4} = 4$. Both sides agree.
Two answers that are not numbers If the variable vanishes and you are left with a true statement like $7 = 7$, every number is a solution — infinitely many solutions. If you are left with a false one like $7 = 3$, there is no solution. Neither means you made a mistake.

L3Inequalities, and the one rule that changes

Inequalities solve exactly like equations, with a single exception that catches everybody at least once.

When you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, the inequality sign flips. $<$ becomes $>$, and $\le$ becomes $\ge$.

Why it flips — watch the number line
-2

The answer to an inequality is not a single number but a set of them, so it deserves a picture. On a number line, an open circle means the endpoint is excluded ($<$ or $>$) and a filled circle means it is included ($\le$ or $\ge$).

1. Solve $3x - 2 > 9 + 5x$ and describe the solution.
Subtract $5x$: $-2x - 2 > 9$.
Add 2: $-2x > 11$.
Divide by $-2$ — flip the sign: $x < -\dfrac{11}{2} = -5.5$.
Every number below $-5.5$ works. Open circle at $-5.5$, arrow to the left.
Check $x = -6$: left side $-20$, right side $-21$, and $-20 > -21$. Correct.
2. Solve $\dfrac{2}{3}x + 9 < 8\left(\dfrac{1}{3}x - 2\right)$.
Distribute: $\dfrac{2}{3}x + 9 < \dfrac{8}{3}x - 16$.
Subtract $\dfrac{2}{3}x$: $9 < 2x - 16$.
Add 16: $25 < 2x$, so $x > 12.5$.
No flip here — we divided by a positive 2. The sign only turns when the divisor is negative.
3. Solve $\dfrac{1}{5}(10 - 20x) \le -14$.
Distribute: $2 - 4x \le -14$.
Subtract 2: $-4x \le -16$.
Divide by $-4$ — flip: $x \ge 4$.
Filled circle at 4, arrow to the right. Check $x = 4$: $\dfrac{1}{5}(10 - 80) = -14$, and $-14 \le -14$ is true.

L4Creating the equation in the first place

Often the hard part is not solving but writing. A routine that works:

1. Name the unknown and state its units. "Let $t$ be the number of tons of wheat."
2. Write the total in words. "Cost of planes plus cost of wheat equals the budget."
3. Replace each phrase with an expression.
4. Solve, then check the answer against the story, not just the algebra.
Budget problem. A government spends $B = xz + yw$, buying $x$ planes at $z$ dollars each and $y$ tons of wheat at $w$ dollars each. It buys 5 planes at \$15 million each and spends \$100 million in total, with wheat at \$300 a ton. How many tons of wheat?
Substituting: $100{,}000{,}000 = 5(15{,}000{,}000) + y(300)$
$100{,}000{,}000 = 75{,}000{,}000 + 300y$
$300y = 25{,}000{,}000$, so $y \approx 83{,}333$ tons.
Check against the story: \$75 million on planes leaves \$25 million, and \$25 million at \$300 a ton is about 83,333 tons. Sensible.
Same formula, different unknown. The government buys 3 planes plus 10,000 tons of wheat at \$500 a ton, spending \$50 million total. What did each plane cost?
$50{,}000{,}000 = 3z + 10{,}000(500)$
$50{,}000{,}000 = 3z + 5{,}000{,}000$
$3z = 45{,}000{,}000$, so $z = \$15{,}000{,}000$ per plane.
The same formula answered a different question just by choosing a different letter to solve for. That is the whole idea of the next section.
Inequalities show up whenever a limit does Words like at most, no more than, at least, and minimum signal an inequality rather than an equation. "A budget of \$1500" means $\text{cost} \le 1500$, not $\text{cost} = 1500$.

L5Rearranging formulas

Solving for a letter when the other letters stay as letters feels different, but it is the same reasoning. Nothing about the rules changes just because the numbers have names.

Solve $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$ for $m$.
Divide both sides by $(x - x_1)$:  $m = \dfrac{y - y_1}{x - x_1}$
Recognise it? That is the slope formula. Point-slope form rearranged is the definition of slope.
Solve $F = \dfrac{9}{5}C + 32$ for $C$.
Subtract 32:  $F - 32 = \dfrac{9}{5}C$
Multiply by $\dfrac{5}{9}$:  $C = \dfrac{5}{9}(F - 32)$
Check with a known pair: $F = 212$ gives $C = \dfrac{5}{9}(180) = 100$. Boiling point, correct.
Pick a formula and a variable to solve for
When a square is involved Solving $S = 4\pi r^2$ for $r$ gives $r = \sqrt{\dfrac{S}{4\pi}}$. Algebraically $\pm$ both work, but $r$ is a radius, so the negative root is discarded. Context decides which solutions survive — a habit worth building now, because Unit 6 is full of it.

L6Solutions you can see

Every point on a graph is a solution

The graph of a two-variable equation is not a decoration of the equation — it is the complete set of its solutions. Every point on the line satisfies the equation, and every point off it does not.

If $(a, b)$ sits on the graph of $y = 2x + 1$, then substituting gives a true statement. If it does not sit on the graph, substituting gives a false one. There is no third option.

Where two graphs cross

Now suppose you want to solve $f(x) = g(x)$. Graph $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$ separately. Wherever they cross, the two outputs are equal at the same input — which is exactly what the equation asks for.

Click anywhere on the grid to test a point against both lines

The x-coordinate is the solution, not the point. If the graphs of $y = 2x+1$ and $y = -x+7$ meet at $(2, 5)$, then the solution of the equation $2x + 1 = -x + 7$ is $x = 2$. The 5 tells you what both sides equal there.

Why this matters beyond straight lines Plenty of equations cannot be solved by algebra you know yet. But if you can graph both sides, you can always read off approximate solutions where the curves cross. NC expects exactly this — approximating with technology or by narrowing down a table of values.

L7Inequalities as regions of the plane

A two-variable equation's solutions form a line. A two-variable inequality's solutions form a whole region on one side of that line.

1. Rearrange into $y = \ldots$ form, remembering to flip the sign if you divide by a negative.
2. Graph the boundary line. Dashed for $<$ or $>$; solid for $\le$ or $\ge$.
3. Shade above for $>$ or $\ge$, below for $<$ or $\le$.
4. Test a point to confirm. $(0,0)$ is easiest, as long as the line does not pass through it.
Build an inequality and see its region
Graph $3x - 4y \le 7$. Solid or dashed? Shade which side?
Rearrange: $-4y \le 7 - 3x$. Divide by $-4$ and flip: $y \ge \dfrac{3x - 7}{4}$.
The sign is $\le$ in the original, so the boundary is solid. After the flip we shade above the line.
Test $(0,0)$ in the original: $3(0) - 4(0) = 0 \le 7$ is true, so the origin is in the region — and it does sit above the line $y = 0.75x - 1.75$. Consistent.
Graph $-9x + 4y \ge 1$. Is the origin a solution?
Rearrange: $4y \ge 9x + 1$, so $y \ge \dfrac{9x + 1}{4}$. No flip — we divided by a positive 4. Solid line, shade above.
Test $(0,0)$: $-9(0) + 4(0) = 0 \ge 1$ is false, so the origin is not a solution and sits outside the shaded region.
This is why testing a point is worth the ten seconds — it catches a flipped shade immediately.

If you remember one thing: check your answer. Substitute back into the original statement, and for word problems check it against the story too. This unit gives partial credit for reasoning, and a checked answer is reasoning you have already done.
