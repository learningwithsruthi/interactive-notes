3.1House vs Senate at a glance

Congress is bicameral: two chambers. This was the Great (Connecticut) Compromise. Big states wanted representation by population, small states wanted equal votes, and the framers gave each side one chamber.

House of Representatives

435 members, seats divided by state population

2-year terms, every seat up every election

Must be 25, a citizen for 7 years

Very structured: strict rules and time limits on debate

Only the House: starts revenue (tax) bills, impeaches (brings charges), picks the president if the Electoral College ties

Senate

100 members, every state gets 2 senators

6-year terms, staggered so one-third are up every two years

Must be 30, a citizen for 9 years

More freedom: unlimited debate, which makes the filibuster possible

Only the Senate: confirms appointments, ratifies treaties (two-thirds), holds impeachment trials (two-thirds to remove)

Why so different? The House was designed to be close to the people: short terms keep members nervous about the next election. The Senate was designed to be the "cooling saucer": long terms and equal state representation slow things down and protect small states.

3.2Two senators each, but House seats by population

Every ten years the census counts everyone, and the 435 House seats are handed out to the states based on population. This is called reapportionment. Every state gets at least one seat. The Senate never changes: every state gets exactly 2, whether it has 39 million people or under 600,000.

Apportionment decides how many seats a state gets. Redistricting is the state then drawing the district lines for those seats, and gerrymandering is drawing them to help one group. Those two are in Topic 6.

3.3Key powers of Congress

These are enumerated powers, listed in Article I, Section 8.

Lawmaking
Writes and passes bills.
War
Declares war; raises and funds an army and navy.
Money
Controls all federal spending and taxing: the power of the purse.
Commerce
Regulates interstate and foreign commerce.
Appointments
The Senate approves or rejects presidential appointments.
Treaties
The Senate ratifies treaties with a two-thirds vote.
Impeachment
The House impeaches; the Senate tries and can remove from office.
The elastic clause The last clause of Section 8, the necessary and proper clause, lets Congress make all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out the listed powers. These unlisted powers are called implied powers, and they are how Congress has stretched its reach over time.

3.4The power of the purse

Congress controls the government through two decisions: how the government gets money (taxes, revenue) and how the government spends it (appropriations). It is also the major way Congress checks the president: no agency can spend a dollar Congress has not approved.

Mandatory spending

Spending already required by an existing law. It happens automatically every year unless Congress changes the law.

Think "must pay."

Examples: Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid.

This is the largest part of the budget, around 60 percent in recent years.

Discretionary spending

Spending Congress decides on each year through the annual budget and appropriations process.

Think "choose to pay."

Examples: defense, education, NASA, national parks.

Defense is the biggest single piece of it.

Sort the spending

Why this matters for AP: since most of the budget is mandatory, Congress only fights over a shrinking slice each year. Changing Social Security or Medicare means passing a new law, which is politically very hard.

3.6House rules vs the Senate filibuster

House: the Rules Committee

Exists only in the House. It decides how a bill will be debated: how long debate lasts and whether amendments can be offered.

Limited debate: members get a set amount of time to speak.

Result: the House can move bills through faster. With 435 members, it has to.

Senate: unlimited debate

Filibuster: a senator (or group) keeps debate going to delay or block a vote. Think "keep talking, slow it down."

Cloture: the vote to end debate and move toward a final vote. It takes 60 senators.

Result: in practice, most major bills need 60 votes in the Senate, not 51.

Exceptions worth knowing Since 2013 and 2017, the Senate needs only a simple majority to end debate on presidential nominations, including Supreme Court justices (often called the "nuclear option"). And certain budget bills, through a process called reconciliation, cannot be filibustered.

3.7Trustee, delegate, politico

When a member of Congress votes, whose opinion counts? AP describes three models.

Trustee
Votes on their own judgment of what is best, even if the district disagrees.
Delegate
Votes the way their constituents want, even against their own view.
Politico
Combines trustee and delegate depending on the issue: follows the district on issues voters care about, uses own judgment on the rest.

3.8Oversight

Oversight is Congress monitoring and investigating the executive branch to make sure laws are being carried out properly. It is the step that happens after a bill becomes a law: Congress watches how the government carries it out.

Hearings
Committees call agency heads to testify and answer questions.
Investigations
Committees can subpoena documents and witnesses.
Budget
Cutting or raising an agency's funding is oversight with teeth.
