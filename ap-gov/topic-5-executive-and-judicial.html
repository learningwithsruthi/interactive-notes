5.1The executive branch: enforces the law

President
Head of state and head of government. Must be 35, a natural-born citizen, and a U.S. resident for 14 years. Four-year terms; the 22nd Amendment limits a president to two elected terms.
Vice President
First in line to succeed the president; breaks ties in the Senate.
Cabinet
The heads of the 15 executive departments (Secretary of State, Secretary of Defense, Attorney General, and so on). Appointed by the president, confirmed by the Senate.
Federal agencies
The bureaucracy that actually carries out laws day to day (FBI, EPA, IRS, FDA, and hundreds more).

5.2Formal and informal powers

Formal powers are written in Article II. Informal powers are not in the Constitution but have grown through practice. AP tests this distinction constantly.

Formal (in the Constitution)

Signs or vetoes bills

Commander in chief of the armed forces

Negotiates treaties (Senate ratifies with two-thirds)

Appoints judges, ambassadors, and Cabinet members (Senate confirms)

Grants pardons and reprieves for federal crimes

Delivers the State of the Union and recommends legislation

Informal (grown through practice)

Executive orders: directives to the executive branch that have the force of law, without Congress

Executive agreements: deals with foreign leaders that do not need Senate ratification

Signing statements: the president's interpretation, written when signing a bill

Bully pulpit: using the attention of the office to push the public and Congress

Executive privilege: keeping some internal communications private

5.4The judicial branch: interprets the law

Article III creates one Supreme Court and lets Congress create lower federal courts. The courts decide what laws mean and whether they were applied fairly, settle disputes between states, and hear cases involving federal law and the Constitution. Through judicial review, they can declare laws and executive actions unconstitutional.

Original jurisdiction
The court hears the case first. For the Supreme Court this is rare: cases between states, or involving ambassadors.
Appellate jurisdiction
The court reviews a lower court's decision. This is almost all of the Supreme Court's work.
Writ of certiorari
An order to send up a case for review. The Court gets thousands of petitions a year and hears only around 60.
Rule of four
Four of the nine justices must agree to hear a case.

5.6Opinions, precedent, activism and restraint

Majority opinion
The official ruling and reasoning, joined by at least five justices. It becomes precedent.
Concurring opinion
Agrees with the outcome but for different or additional reasons.
Dissenting opinion
Disagrees with the outcome. Dissents have no legal force, but future courts sometimes adopt their reasoning.
Stare decisis
"Let the decision stand." Courts generally follow precedent, so the law stays predictable.
Judicial activism
Willingness to overturn precedent or strike down laws and actions of the other branches.
Judicial restraint
Deference to the elected branches and to precedent; striking things down only when clearly unconstitutional.
The court's weakness (back to Federalist 78) Courts cannot enforce their own rulings. They rely on the executive branch to carry them out and on public trust in their legitimacy. That is what Hamilton meant by "neither force nor will, but merely judgment."
