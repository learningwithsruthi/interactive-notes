1.1What the Federalist Papers were for

In 1787 the Constitution was written, but it was not law yet. Nine states had to ratify it. New York was a tough sell, so Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay wrote 85 newspaper essays under the shared pen name "Publius" to argue for it. Those essays are the Federalist Papers.

The people on the other side were the Anti-Federalists. They worried the new national government would swallow the states and turn into the same kind of tyranny the colonies had just fought a war to escape. Every Federalist paper is, in some way, an answer to that fear.

The trick to reading any Federalist paper: ask "what is the Anti-Federalist worried about here, and how is Publius calming them down?" In Federalist 70 the worry is a president who becomes a king. In Federalist 78 the worry is unelected judges with too much power.

What it is actually about

  • Unitary executive: power belongs to one person, not a council.
  • Defense: the country needs someone who can act fast against foreign threats.
  • Energy: an energetic executive is needed to deal with foreign nations and enforce the laws.
  • Accountability: with one person in charge, voters know exactly who to blame.
Federalist 78

What I know

An independent judiciary to protect our rights. Judicial review is the ability to say a law is unconstitutional. A judge's term is their whole life.

Click to flip

What it is actually about

  • Federal judges keep their offices "during good behaviour", which in practice means for life.
  • Lifetime appointments let judges rule without worrying about pleasing voters to get re-elected.
  • The judiciary is the least dangerous branch: no army, no money, only judgment.
  • Judicial review does not make judges superior to Congress. The power of the people, written into the Constitution, is superior to both.

1.3Federalist 70: energy in the executive

Under the Articles of Confederation there was no president at all. Congress tried to run everything by committee, and it was slow and weak. Hamilton's thesis is the first line you should memorize:

"Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government."Federalist 70, Alexander Hamilton (1788)

"Energy" here means the ability to act: decisively, quickly, and with enough power to actually get things done. Hamilton lists four ingredients.

1. Unity
One person holds executive power. This is the heart of the essay.
2. Duration
A term long enough (four years) to carry out plans without constantly worrying about the next election.
3. Support
A salary Congress cannot cut, so Congress cannot bully the president with money.
4. Competent powers
Real authority: veto, commander in chief, appointments, treaties.

Why one person instead of a council?

"Decision, activity, secrecy, and dispatch will generally characterize the proceedings of one man in a much more eminent degree than the proceedings of any greater number."Federalist 70

"Dispatch" means speed. Hamilton's second reason is about blame. When a group shares power, every member can point at the others, so a plural executive "tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility." With one president, the public knows exactly who made the call.

1.4Federalist 78: the least dangerous branch

Anti-Federalists feared unelected federal judges who could not be removed by voters. Hamilton's answer starts by comparing what each branch controls.

"The executive not only dispenses the honors, but holds the sword of the community. The legislature not only commands the purse, but prescribes the rules... The judiciary, on the contrary, has no influence over either the sword or the purse... It may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment."Federalist 78

Point 1: life tenure keeps judges independent

Federal judges serve "during good behaviour," which means they stay until they die, retire, or are impeached and removed. Hamilton argues nothing "can contribute so much to its firmness and independence as permanency in office." A judge who never faces re-election can rule against a popular law, or a popular president, without fearing for their job.

Point 2: courts can declare laws unconstitutional

"The interpretation of the laws is the proper and peculiar province of the courts." If a law passed by Congress conflicts with the Constitution, the judge has to follow the Constitution, because the Constitution is the higher law. Hamilton calls courts "the bulwarks of a limited Constitution against legislative encroachments." This is the idea of judicial review. The Court will formally claim it fifteen years later in Marbury v. Madison (Topic 6).

Point 3: this does not make judges the boss

"Nor does this conclusion by any means suppose a superiority of the judicial to the legislative power. It only supposes that the power of the people is superior to both."Federalist 78

Translate it: the Constitution is the people's will. A statute is only the legislature's will. When they clash, a judge siding with the Constitution is siding with the people, not with themselves.
