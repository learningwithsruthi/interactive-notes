6.1Three words that sound alike

Apportionment
Dividing the 435 House seats among the states after each census. Decides how many seats a state gets. (You played with this in Topic 3.)
Redistricting
Each state redrawing its district lines so each district has roughly the same population. In most states, the state legislature draws the lines; some states use independent commissions.
Gerrymandering
Drawing district lines on purpose to help one party or group. The name comes from Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry, whose 1812 district was shaped like a salamander.
Packing
Cramming the other side's voters into a few districts they win by huge margins, wasting their extra votes.
Cracking
Splitting the other side's voters across many districts so they are a losing minority in each.

6.2Gerrymandering lab

This state has 50 voters and needs 5 districts of 10 voters each. 60% support Party A (blue circles) and 40% support Party B (gold squares). Same voters every time. Only the lines change.

What to notice: with 60% of the vote, Party A can end up with 5 seats, 3 seats, or just 2 seats, depending only on who draws the map. Every one of those maps has districts with exactly equal populations, so they all satisfy "one person, one vote." Equal population alone does not stop gerrymandering.

6.3Marbury v. Madison (1803)

The one-line version: Marbury = the Court gets the power of judicial review. Click through the story.

Why it was a brilliant move: Marshall gave Jefferson's side the win (Marbury got nothing), so there was no order for Jefferson to defy. But in exchange, the Court claimed a far bigger power: the right to strike down acts of Congress. This is the formal start of what Hamilton described in Federalist 78.

6.4Baker v. Carr (1962)

The one-line version: Baker = one person, one vote.

Facts
Tennessee had not redrawn its state legislative districts since 1901, about 60 years, even though huge numbers of people had moved to cities. Rural districts with few people had the same representation as packed urban districts. Charles Baker, a voter from Shelby County (Memphis), sued.
Question
Is redistricting a "political question" courts must stay out of, or is it justiciable (something a court can decide)?
Holding (6 to 2)
It is justiciable. Unequal districts can violate the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment, so federal courts can hear these cases.
Significance
Opened the door to the "one person, one vote" standard (applied in Reynolds v. Sims, 1964): districts must have roughly equal populations so every vote carries equal weight.

6.5Shaw v. Reno (1993)

The one-line version: Shaw = no racial gerrymandering; race-based districts must pass strict scrutiny.

Facts
After the 1990 census, North Carolina's population growth earned it a 12th House seat. Under the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the U.S. Justice Department pushed the state to create a second majority-Black district. The resulting 12th District snaked about 160 miles along Interstate 85, in places no wider than the highway. Five white voters sued.
Question
Does a district drawn mainly on the basis of race violate the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment?
Holding (5 to 4)
A district so bizarrely shaped that it can only be explained by race can be challenged as a racial gerrymander, and it must pass strict scrutiny. The case was sent back to the lower court; in a follow-up case (Shaw v. Hunt, 1996) the district failed strict scrutiny and was struck down.
Significance
Race cannot be the main factor in drawing district lines unless the state can meet the highest standard of review.
Strict scrutiny
The toughest test a law can face. The government must show a compelling interest and that the law is narrowly tailored (no less race-based way would work). Most laws fail it.
Beyond the board: partisan gerrymandering Shaw is about racial gerrymandering. For partisan gerrymandering (like the lab above), the Court ruled in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) that it is a political question federal courts will not decide. Voters, state courts, and state laws (like independent commissions) are left to handle it.
