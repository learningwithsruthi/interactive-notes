How a Bill Becomes Law

Walk a bill from introduction to law, and see every place along the way where it can quietly die.

MapWhat this topic covers

Thousands of bills are introduced in every two-year Congress. Only a few percent become law. The process is built with many places to say no, which is the whole point: it is checks and balances in slow motion.

4.1The seven steps

1. The bill is introduced in the House or the Senate by a member of that chamber. Exception: revenue (tax) bills must start in the House.
2. Committee review. The bill goes to a committee, which holds hearings, edits it (markup), and votes. Most bills die here.
3. Floor debate and vote. In the House, the Rules Committee sets the rules for debate. In the Senate, debate is unlimited, so a bill may face a filibuster.
4. Sent to the other chamber. The whole committee and floor process repeats.
5. Conference committee. If the two chambers passed different versions, members of both meet to reconcile them into one bill.
6. Both chambers pass the identical bill by a simple majority in each.
7. Goes to the president: sign, veto, or take no action.

After that, the job is not over. Through oversight, Congress monitors and investigates the executive branch to make sure the new law is being carried out properly.

4.3The president's four options

When the bill reaches the president, the clock starts: 10 days, not counting Sundays.

Sign itIt becomes law.
Veto itIt goes back to Congress with the president's objections. Congress can override with two-thirds of both chambers. Otherwise it dies.
No action, Congress in sessionAfter 10 days it becomes law without a signature.
No action, Congress adjournsIf Congress adjourns before the 10 days are up, the bill dies. This is a pocket veto, and Congress cannot override it.
A common mistake "If the president does nothing for 10 days it becomes law" is only true while Congress is in session. If Congress has adjourned, doing nothing kills the bill.

4.5Vocabulary AP loves

Markup
When a committee edits and amends a bill line by line.
Pigeonholing
A committee (usually its chair) sets a bill aside and never acts on it. The bill quietly dies.
Discharge petition
In the House, if a majority (218) of members sign, a bill is forced out of a committee that is sitting on it.
Filibuster / cloture
Senate delay through unlimited debate / the 60-vote motion to end it.
Conference committee
A temporary committee of members from both chambers that writes a single compromise version.
Pork barrel
Spending for projects in a member's home district (often delivered through earmarks).
Logrolling
"I vote for your bill if you vote for mine." Trading votes.
Rider
An unrelated amendment attached to a bill that is likely to pass.
Pocket veto
The president does nothing and Congress adjourns within 10 days, so the bill dies.
